01 · Introduction

Understanding an agent takes more than recognizing a reaction

A character approaches an object, recoils, and stops. Does it dislike the object, lack the ability to pass it, or pause before trying another route? A recognizable reaction can express fear without revealing a capability limit or an intended destination.

Peter Pan is a projected shadow-agent system: users change the physical scene and watch what the character does next. Three perceived qualities are examined — behavioral continuity, capability awareness, and goal-directed planning — by comparing rule-based planning with role- and context-conditioned LLM planning.

RQ1 How do perceived goals and capabilities differ between the two planners during exploration? RQ2 How do they differ in capability awareness and continuity under task constraints? RQ3 Which behavioral cues do users rely on, and where do interpretations stay uncertain?

02 · System

From scene change to projected behavior

A camera segments the scene (SAM2) and a vision-language model labels objects. These become a structured environment description on a shared 2D world plane, which a planner uses to choose among four preset actions — Walking, Jumping, Scared, Liking — rendered as a projected silhouette.

RULE picks actions with ten ordered rules based on geometry, reachability and events. LLM additionally receives semantic scene information and a role with dispositions (e.g. fear of insects, liked objects), with a shorter decision cooldown (3 s vs 5 s) and capped expressions in Study 1.

03 · Study

Pilot with experts, then an 18-person study

Eight architecture and design experts tried the first prototype. Three issues shaped the design: inaccurate hand detection blurred which intervention caused a response; sticky notes looked like special system markers; and people spontaneously built barriers to test what the character could do.

In the main study, every participant experienced both planners in two tasks (~55 min): Study 1 environmental exploration, and Study 2 an agent-only apple task under changing constraints. Participants were not told which planner they saw or that an apple target existed. Measures were questionnaire ratings (A/B/C, Godspeed, Raw-TLX), event descriptions, and a closing interview.

04 · Findings

Clear gains in exploration, no consistent difference under constraints

During exploration, LLM planning received significantly higher ratings for goal-directed planning (5.16 vs 4.14) and capability awareness (5.02 vs 3.71). Under task constraints, neither capability awareness nor behavioral continuity differed consistently, and individual participants favored both directions.

Interviews and event descriptions separated three things users read from behavior: a visible reaction, an interpretable capability boundary, and a recognizable tendency across change. A character that jumped did not necessarily reveal where it was going, and an unexplained stop could mean inability, reluctance, or loading.

05 · Implications

Making reactions, limits and continuity legible

The findings motivate three design directions for human–agent interaction: connect immediate reactions to ongoing goals; keep the difference between inability and reluctance readable; and show what persists through environmental change. Limitations include a small sample, a configuration comparison that bundled decision strategy, semantic information and timing, and an undisclosed task goal.
